# Daily Standup Tips: How to Run Standups That Don't Waste Time

The daily standup is the most frequent Scrum ceremony — and the one most likely to go wrong. When it works, it's a 15-minute sync that keeps the team aligned and unblocks work. When it doesn't, it's a dreaded status meeting that eats into productive time.

Here's how to make your standups worth attending.

## The Purpose of a Daily Standup

The standup exists to answer one question: **Is the team on track to meet the sprint goal?**

It's not a status report for managers. It's not a problem-solving session. It's a quick synchronization so team members know what's happening, can spot blockers early, and can coordinate their work for the day.

## The Classic Format

Each team member answers three questions:

1. **What did I complete yesterday?** (not "work on" — *complete*)
2. **What will I complete today?**
3. **What's blocking me?**

This format works, but it has a failure mode: people start reporting to the Scrum Master instead of talking to each other. The questions should spark conversation, not rote recitation.

### An Alternative: Walk the Board

Instead of going person-by-person, walk through the sprint board from right to left (closest to done → just started). For each in-progress item:

- What's the status?
- What's needed to move it forward?
- Who's working on it today?

This keeps the focus on *work flow* rather than individual status, which is what actually matters for the sprint goal.

## Keep It to 15 Minutes

The standup has a strict time limit for a reason. Here's how to enforce it:

### Use a Timer

A per-person timer keeps everyone concise. Aim for **90 seconds per person** — with a team of 7, that's about 10 minutes of updates plus buffer for quick questions. A [standup timer](/daily-standup) with per-member tracking makes this easy.

### Take It Offline

The magic phrase: **"Let's take that offline."** Any topic that only involves 2-3 people or requires more than 30 seconds of discussion should be parked. Have the relevant people stay after standup to discuss in detail.

### Stand Up (Really)

If you're in person, actually stand. It's not just tradition — physical discomfort is a natural time limiter. Nobody wants to have a 30-minute discussion while standing.

### Same Time, Same Place

Consistency removes friction. If standup is at 9:30 every day in the same room (or video call), people build a habit and you don't waste time coordinating logistics.

## Common Problems and Fixes

### Problem: It's a Status Report

**Symptom**: Team members address the Scrum Master or manager instead of each other. Updates sound rehearsed and nobody is listening to each other.

**Fix**: The Scrum Master should physically step back or stay silent. Frame updates as "what does the team need to know?" Have team members face each other, not the Scrum Master.

### Problem: It Takes 30+ Minutes

**Symptom**: Discussions go deep on technical details. One person's blocker turns into a group debugging session.

**Fix**: Use a timer. Enforce the "take it offline" rule aggressively. If deep discussions keep happening, it may mean the team needs a separate daily technical sync.

### Problem: People Show Up Late

**Symptom**: The standup starts 5 minutes late every day while waiting for stragglers.

**Fix**: Start on time, every time. If someone is late, they've missed their turn. The team shouldn't be penalized for one person's schedule.

### Problem: It Feels Pointless

**Symptom**: Team members see it as a chore. Updates are generic ("worked on the same thing as yesterday").

**Fix**: Try the Walk the Board format. Focus on sprint goal progress rather than individual tasks. If the team is truly well-synchronized (e.g., pairing constantly), consider reducing frequency.

### Problem: Too Many People

**Symptom**: With 12+ people, the standup takes forever and most information isn't relevant to most attendees.

**Fix**: Split into smaller team standups. Observers (stakeholders, managers) should listen silently or read async updates instead.

## Remote Standup Tips

Remote standups have unique challenges — and some surprising advantages.

### Video On, Distractions Off

Cameras help people stay engaged. Close Slack, email, and your IDE. A 15-minute meeting only works if everyone is actually present for those 15 minutes.

### Consider Async Standups

For distributed teams across time zones, synchronous standups may not work. An async standup (written updates posted in a Slack channel or tool) can be just as effective:

- Post your three answers at the start of your workday
- Read your teammates' updates
- Flag any blockers or coordination needs

The tradeoff is less spontaneous discussion, but the information still flows.

### Use Visual Tools

Screen-share the sprint board during standup. Visual progress tracking keeps the discussion anchored. A [standup timer](/daily-standup) displayed on screen adds helpful time pressure.

## Advanced Patterns

### Sprint Goal Focus

Start the standup with: "Our sprint goal is X. Here's where we are." This orients every update around what matters. If someone's work isn't related to the sprint goal, that's worth discussing.

### Blocker Board

Maintain a visible list of current blockers. At each standup, quickly review: are any resolved? Any new ones? This prevents blockers from being mentioned once and then forgotten.

### Walking Skeleton

For new features, track daily progress on the end-to-end flow. "Yesterday the API returned data. Today the frontend will display it." This gives the team a shared mental model of how the feature is coming together.

## Measuring Standup Effectiveness

You'll know your standups are working when:

- They consistently finish in under 15 minutes
- Team members actively coordinate during or right after the standup
- Blockers are raised and resolved quickly
- Nobody dreads attending

If you want quantitative tracking, measure sprint velocity trends — effective standups contribute to higher, more consistent [team velocity](/velocity) by catching blockers early and improving coordination.
